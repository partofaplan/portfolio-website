What I actually believe

Four things I keep coming back to

These are not aspirations posted on a wall. Each one came out of getting it wrong first, and each one shows up in the projects and the writing further down.

01

Iterate. Don't heroically rebuild.

The instinct to break your back on a problem is not the same as solving it. I sort work into four buckets now: quick wins that unblock someone today, easy fixes with hard implications that need consensus first, real problems that belong to somebody else's growth, and the hero trap — the ones I want to solve because saving the day feels good.

"The real skill isn't in solving every problem. It's in recognizing which problems deserve your energy and which ones deserve your restraint."

02

Learn by standing it up and breaking it.

I have nine sandbox repositories and not one of them was an assignment. KEDA, Bedrock, agentic loops, local voice models, PVC behavior, Rancher's audit internals. Reading the docs tells you what a tool claims. Standing it up, pushing until it fails, and writing down where it bit tells you what it actually is — and that is the knowledge worth having when it matters at 3 a.m.

03

Elevate the developer experience, or don't bother.

A platform nobody adopts is a hobby. If an engineer is sitting idle waiting on an environment, that is my defect, not theirs — and "it takes two days to get a dev environment" is a problem you delete rather than document. Both kad and kado-operator exist for exactly that reason.

04

Make the system tell you the truth.

Applications that ship without signals do not tell anyone they are sick — they just look fine until production disagrees. Feedback loops belong embedded at every step of promotion: the playground, the proving ground, and the show ground. QA is not a discipline adjacent to development; it is the spine of it.

"What is not seen is not quantified, and when it is not quantified we cannot act."

Right now Platform architecture for a Department of Defense program at Aviture — self-managed RKE2/Rancher clusters in an airgapped government cloud, supporting mission-critical Java workloads. Which is a long way of saying: the constraints are real, and the feedback loops have to be better because of it.

kado-operator

Go

Development environments as a custom resource.

A Kubernetes operator that provisions and reclaims an entire development environment from one CRD. A DevEnvironment owns a namespace and everything in it — a ConfigMap built from spec, copies of referenced Secrets, a shared PVC, and a Deployment plus ClusterIP Service per requested backing service. Services find each other by name. A finalizer takes the whole namespace back down on delete, so nothing leaks and nothing lingers on the bill.

kad

Go

An instant Kubernetes dev environment, from one file.

The local counterpart: a single static binary with no runtime dependencies that builds a minikube cluster to a declaration your team checks in, installs the tooling and backing services, tells you how to reach them, and deletes all of it again. kad doctor budgets the cluster against the container runtime rather than the host, so it fails with a useful message before minikube does — not thirty seconds after.

rancher-audit-log-operator

Go

Turning Rancher UI audit JSON into sentences a human can read.

Rancher's UI audit trail is an application-level log, not kube-apiserver audit, and raw it is close to unreadable. A Kubebuilder operator reconciles a Filebeat shipper from an AuditLogConfig CR, and each entry is enriched at ingest into an audit.summary sentence — zperkins create deployments monitoring/web — so "who did what, when" is one Kibana query. Ships as a local two-cluster sandbox and a Helm-deployed production path into an airgapped cluster, with a documented handoff package for the ELK team.

postgres-delta

Helm

HA PostgreSQL that reconfigures without a restart.

Patroni-managed PostgreSQL 16 with streaming replicas and automatic failover, packaged as a chart. The interesting part is the upgrade path: change patroni.postgresql.parameters, run helm upgrade, and a post-upgrade hook pushes the rendered config through the Patroni API so PostgreSQL reloads — no pod restarts, no failover, no downtime. A PodDisruptionBudget and soft anti-affinity keep quorum intact through node maintenance.

airgap-postgres

Helm

A database cluster that installs with the internet unplugged.

PostgreSQL 18 in HA behind a pair of HAProxy load balancers — port 5432 routes to the primary for writes, 5433 spreads reads across every node — with hourly CronJob backups and scripted recovery. Every image resolves to a single registry, which is the whole point: in a disconnected environment, one chart that never reaches out is worth more than a dozen that almost do.

voice-ai-sandbox

TypeScript

A complete voice loop where every stage is swappable.

Mic capture gated on silence, then speech-to-text, then an LLM, then text-to-speech, then playback. Each stage is an engine selected by environment variable, so the identical loop runs fully offline on whisper.cpp, Ollama, and Kokoro, or hybrid on Deepgram, Claude, and ElevenLabs. Built to answer a practical question: how much of a voice assistant can you actually run with no network at all?

Talk 2025

The Key Is Culture

Why it takes a growth mindset to combat organizational entropy

Organizational entropy is the gradual decline of teams and systems into disorder. Knowledge silos form, bottlenecks appear, the hero mentality takes over, and standards quietly stop existing. The talk argues these behaviors are not character flaws but adaptive responses to a broken system — and that culture is process + people + purpose, something you architect deliberately rather than something you hope for.

"Today's brilliant, Red Bull-slinging cowboy engineer is tomorrow's 'idiot who built this thing.'"
Lightning talk 2026

Seeing Is Believing

Feedback loops in DevOps, and why validation matters

Opens at 3 a.m. with a page, a cache filling a production disk, and an app that looked perfectly healthy in every lower environment — because the cache was traffic-generated and nothing downstream of dev ever generated traffic. From there it names the DevOps death spiral: bandaid, forget, inherit, repeat. The fix is structural — the playground, the proving ground, and the show ground, each with feedback loops embedded rather than bolted on.

"What is not seen is not quantified, and when it is not quantified we cannot act. QA is not a separate discipline from development — it is the spine of development."
Talk 2025

The Power of Mission

Goals get you to a finish line. Missions give you a reason to keep running.

On the difference between a goal — specific, measurable, short-term — and a mission, which is long-term and identity-oriented. Employees are surrounded by deadlines, KPIs, and performance reviews; what they are actually looking for is something bigger than themselves. Closes on a line worth keeping: people don't want to just succeed at work, they want to matter.

Essay 2026

The Four Problem Buckets

A framework for triage, written after the burnout that comes from saying yes to everything. Tackle it now — quick wins that unblock someone. Easy fix, hard implications — the deceptive ones that ripple through five other teams. Not now, but later — real problems that belong to someone else's growth. And the hero trap, the hardest bucket, where you want to save the day but restraint is the more valuable skill.

"The real skill isn't in solving every problem. It's in recognizing which problems deserve your energy and which ones deserve your restraint."
Essay 2026

What DevOps Actually Means

DevOps started as a post-Agile movement to reduce friction between development and operations, then became a job title, and is now a moving target. This piece stakes out a definition borrowed from Google's SRE book and argues the disciplines have converged on one goal — take problems and solve them with code — which requires front-loading planning and sandboxing instead of breaking your back on the problem in front of you.

"Work smarter, not harder. Once the planning stage is complete, we get to run fast with solutions we already know work."
Engineering standards 2025

Platform Engineering Best Practices

The internal standards document adopted across teams: base images versioned in code and patched only through a pipeline; protected trunk with two approvers and test criteria on every merge request; no merge request older than a sprint, because code gets stale; deployments through versioned Helm releases only, with rollback to the last revision; and application monitors that account for every dependent connection, not just the web tier.

Decks and drafts available on request — several were delivered internally and are not yet published.
